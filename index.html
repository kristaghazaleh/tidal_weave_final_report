Abstract

Tidal Weave is a real-time ocean surface simulation and rendering project grounded in Tessendorf’s spectral ocean framework. Our goal was to build a system that combines believable open-ocean motion with a water appearance driven by surface response rather than a static textured plane. The final project is organized around three tightly connected parts: surface geometry, water shading, and presentation of the rendered scene.

Over the course of the project, we built a stable OpenGL renderer, a tiled ocean patch, a displacement pipeline, and a water shading prototype that made the surface read more convincingly as water through displaced normals, view-dependent response, and overall scene framing. The most important lesson from the project was that believable water depends on geometry and shading working together: a moving height field alone is not enough, and the surface only starts to feel convincing once motion, normals, and rendering choices reinforce each other. Our final result is a working ocean prototype that demonstrates this combined pipeline and documents the path from early mesh rendering to a much stronger water-surface appearance.

Technical Approach

Geometry and surface construction

The simulation is built around a tiled ocean patch that provides the basic mesh structure for the surface. From there, the renderer updates the patch over time through animated displacement, giving the system a working ocean surface backbone before the full spectral simulation is inserted. This phased approach let us validate the renderer, mesh generation, and update loop before moving further into more advanced surface behavior.

In the current implementation, the displacement stage establishes the evolving surface and gives the renderer a stable place to later connect the full spectral formulation. This meant that even when the project was still in prototype form, the geometry path was already structured around the eventual simulation goal rather than being a one-off visual effect.

Shading and rendering

The second major part of the project is the water shader. Its role is to turn the animated surface into something that visually reads as water through the use of displaced-surface normals, color response, and view-dependent behavior. Through iteration, we found that the surface only began to look believable once the geometry and shading were coupled more carefully, especially when the camera angle and the surface-response logic started to reinforce the shape of the waves rather than flatten them.

Problems encountered and how we addressed them

One recurring challenge was that early prototypes could move, but still did not look convincingly like water. A second issue was integrating shader files and renderer behavior in a way that stayed stable while we iterated. We addressed these problems by building the project in phases, validating each layer separately, and adjusting the surface shading only after the renderer and displacement pipeline were already reliable. This made it much easier to distinguish setup problems from visual problems.

Lessons learned

The biggest lesson from the project is that water appearance depends on more than motion. Surface slope, normals, camera angle, and shading behavior matter just as much as displacement itself. We also learned that phased prototyping was the right strategy: validating the mesh and renderer first made later shader development much more manageable and gave us a cleaner path toward the final spectral simulation.
